I spent half the day on a high-speed train, still trying to understand GADTs in OCaml. By evening, I'd checked into my accommodation in Zhujiajiao, Shanghai.

I went out for a walk alone that night. For some reason, the place felt a lot like the town described in CLANNAD. There were plenty of little restaurants and one or two medium-sized shopping malls, while other parts still had the look of an old village. Streetlights dotted the night, and cars were few and far between.

I wondered what it would be like in real life to live in a town like this from birth, as in the game: slowly growing up, getting married, having children, and growing old.

I kept walking and thinking, and eventually came back to the same feeling: reality never quite offers such a pure, secluded idyll.

Historically, in places far from population centers, peace, stability, and material abundance seem to belong to rare periods. Look back just twenty years, perhaps, and people were still struggling to put food on the table.

Technologically, the world has long since been folded into our phones, into high-speed trains and airplanes. People's feelings are no longer so innocent. Even after parting, a video call, or a train or plane ticket, can bring us together again before long. There seems less need for the old lament that parting has always wounded the tenderhearted.

As for people's aspirations, in Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai, the familiar expectations are always to distinguish yourself through exams, head for a big city, and battle for money and status in the city center. Settling quietly in one corner is more often seen as giving up and letting things slide.

Those flawless feelings and stories are ultimately imaginary. Living in this world, we will always have regrets, always yearn for the paths we didn't take. Living in the present, feeling more of what is here and now, may be a provisional answer.
